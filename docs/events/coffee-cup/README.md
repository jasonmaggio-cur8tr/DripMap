## 1. Why this exists

Three things happen at once, and only one of them looks like a soccer tournament.

1. **Sacramento's coffee scene gets a rivalry.** Shops that have never met each other's staff now have a shared Sunday and a trophy to fight over. That's a community, not an event.
2. **Futsi fills an off-peak Sunday** with 200+ people who have never walked through its doors, and converts a slice of them into league teams.
3. **DripMap onboards 12 shop owners in person, in one afternoon,** with a reason to care. Every registration runs through DripMap. Every shop claims its profile. That is the actual point.

Treat #3 as the KPI. The tournament is the excuse.

**Year-one success looks like:**
- 12 shops registered and claimed on DripMap
- 200+ spectators through the door
- 1 title sponsor covering the shirt print run
- 1 highlight video + a photo folder per shop delivered within 48 hours
- Futsi asks when the next one is before you leave the building

## 2. The trophy is the product

Spend real money here. Everything else is logistics.

**The Coffee Cup** is an oversized cup on a hardwood base with engraved plates for the next ten winners. It is *perpetual* — the winning shop keeps it on their bar until someone takes it from them. Budget $250–350 and order it in week 2, because engravers take three weeks.

This is the single line item that makes year two inevitable. A shop with a trophy on their bar for twelve months is a twelve-month advertisement for the next one, and every shop that walks in and sees it wants to enter.

## 3. Format

### Structure — 12 teams, 2 courts

- **Group stage:** 3 groups of 4, full round robin. Every team plays **3 guaranteed games**.
- **Knockout:** 3 group winners + best runner-up → semifinals → 3rd place → **the Final on one court with everyone around it**.
- **Total:** 21 games. 18 group + 2 semis + 3rd place + Final.

Every team gets at least three games for their $100. Finalists get five. That is the value story when a shop asks "what do I actually get for a Sunday?"

### If Futsi only has one court

Cap the field at **8 teams** (2 groups of 4, semis, final = 15 games) and say so in the invite. Do not try to run 12 teams on one court — 21 games at 14-minute slots is five hours of soccer and you will lose the crowd, the light and the goodwill somewhere around game 14.

**Confirm court count with Futsi before you pitch a single shop.**

### Game rules — keep it to one page

| Rule | Spec |
|---|---|
| Players | 3 on the court. No dedicated goalkeepers, small goals, no hands. |
| Game length | Two 6-minute halves, running clock, 1-minute half. 14-minute slot. |
| Knockouts | Two 7-minute halves. 16-minute slot. |
| Roster | 5–8 players. Minimum 2 women. Minimum 2 shop staff/owners. |
| Gender rule | **At least one woman on the court at all times.** Nonbinary players count toward this at their own discretion. |
| Short-handed | Can't field a woman? Play 2v3. You are never forfeited for it. |
| Subs | Unlimited, rolling, at the halfway line. |
| Restarts | Kick-ins, not throw-ins. 4 seconds to restart. |
| Safety | No slide tackles. Indoor shoes or flats only, no studs. |
| Discipline | 2-minute sin bin for dissent or reckless play. Ref's call is final. |
| Group ties | Goal difference → goals scored → head-to-head → coin flip at the trophy table. |
| Knockout ties | 3 minutes golden goal, then 3-round shootout. Three different shooters, at least one a woman. |

**Two rules worth defending when someone pushes back:**

- **Minimum 2 women on the roster, not 1.** If a team rosters one woman and she rolls an ankle in game one, you've turned an inclusion rule into a forfeit. Two is what makes the rule work in practice, and it means she gets to rest.
- **Minimum 2 shop staff on the roster.** You said community players are welcome and they should be — but if a shop shows up with five strangers in their apron colors, it stops being the coffee scene playing soccer and becomes a rec league with sponsors. Two staff is a low bar that keeps it honest.

Run a **player pool** for the gender rule rather than letting shops scramble: collect names of women who want to play but aren't attached to a shop, and match them to the teams who need one. It's a generous move, it fills rosters, and it's how you get ten people who love the event but don't work in coffee.

## 5. Money

### The registration fee question — take the $100

You asked $90 flat versus $100 with three tees. **$100 with three tees, and here's why:** $90 is a fee. $100 with merch is a *package*, and the tee is a souvenir that walks around Sacramento for two years. Nobody remembers paying ten dollars less. Everybody remembers the shirt.

**But change one thing: collect it at registration, not on the day.**

Day-of cash is the single biggest leak in this plan. You will be running a bracket, and instead you'll be chasing twelve people with a Venmo QR code while a semifinal waits. Worse: your shirts go to print on Oct 28 with twelve shop names on the back. If one shop no-shows on Nov 15, you've already paid for their name and you have no money and no leverage.

Stripe is already wired into DripMap. Registration is a checkout. It takes an afternoon to build and it means:
- The field is real when you say it's real
- Every registering shop touches DripMap, which is the whole strategic point
- Nov 15 you run a tournament instead of a collections desk

If a shop genuinely can't pay up front, take the $100 on the day from *that* shop and move on. One exception, handled by you, is not a policy.

### The shirt

Classic tour-tee. Front: small left chest — `THE COFFEE CUP · SACRAMENTO · 11.15.26`. Back: all twelve shop names stacked in a tight list, DripMap × Futsi lockup at the bottom. One-color print on a heavyweight cream or dark tee.

Three included per shop. **Sell extras at $30** — pre-order at registration and a table on the day. Rosters run 5–8, so most teams buy 3–5 more, and spectators buy them too. Print ~40 extra. That's where the shirts stop costing you money and start making it.

Go get one. Put their mark on the back of the tee under the shop list and on the bracket board. Ask for $1,500, settle at $1,000.

## 6. Risks worth naming

1. **Sunday afternoon is a work shift for coffee people.** Most Sac shops close between 2 and 5 on a Sunday, and openers started at 5am. A 1:00pm first whistle excludes exactly the people you want playing. **Start at 2:00pm.** Ask your first three shops to sanity-check that and move it if they say 3.
2. **Futsi's Sunday may already be booked** with their own adult leagues. Everything downstream depends on this. Call before you pitch anyone.
3. **Seven weeks is enough only if the venue locks in week one.** The trophy needs three weeks, the shirts need two, and the shops need two to decide. The critical path runs through Futsi.
4. **Shops drop.** Pitch 18–20 to land 12. Keep two on a standby list through Oct 26.
5. **Injuries.** Adults, indoors, 3v3, competitive — you will see a rolled ankle and possibly a calf. Non-negotiables: a digital waiver signed by every player at registration, a first aid kit, ice on site, and written confirmation that Futsi's insurance covers a private ticketed-adjacent event. Ask Futsi for their certificate of insurance and whether you need your own rider. Do not skip this because it's a friendly game.
6. **The gender rule can read as tokenism if you let it.** Run the player pool, put women on the flyer and in the photos, and make sure the Golden Bean and the Crema are open to everyone. It's a good rule — implement it like you mean it.

## 7. After the final whistle

The 48 hours after matter almost as much as the day.

- **Within 24 hours:** recap post, bracket result graphic, and the Crema vote live on Instagram while people are still buzzing.
- **Within 48 hours:** a photo folder per shop, delivered to the owner, free to use forever. This is the thing they'll remember and it costs you nothing.
- **Within a week:** short survey to captains (what worked, what didn't, would you pay $150 next time), thank-you to Futsi with attendance and social numbers, and a Futsi league sign-up push to everyone who played.
- **Announce the next one before the goodwill cools.** A spring edition, or better — the **Coffee League**, a six-week Wednesday-night coffee shop league at Futsi. That's recurring revenue for Futsi, recurring content for DripMap, and it's the reason Futsi should say yes to year one.

And then it goes to other cities. The Coffee Cup in Portland, Seattle, Oakland, LA — same format, same trophy design, local venue partner. That's not an event anymore, that's a DripMap franchise.

## Sponsor tiers

Defined in the partner deck; repeated here so the numbers stay in one place.

| Tier | Price | Availability | What it carries |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Presenting Partner** | $1,500 | 1, category exclusive | Name in the event title, top billing on the back of every shirt, the banner and the bracket board, MC mentions, a table on site, a line in all 12 shops' posts |
| **Court Sponsor** | $750 | 2 | A court carries their name all day and is announced on every game called there. Banner, shirt placement, recap inclusion. |
| **Cup Sponsor** | $250 | Open | Bracket board, table tents, recap post mention |

**Reach model for the sponsor conversation** — roughly 80,000–150,000 year-one impressions: ~27,000 from the 12 shops' own social (12 × ~5,000 followers × 3 posts at ~15% organic reach), ~50,000 from the commemorative tee over two years, ~250 high-dwell on-site impressions across four hours, plus DripMap's channels, a livestream of the final and 1–2 local press placements. At $1,500 that is about a $15 CPM.

Lead with the endorsement, not the CPM. A $15 CPM reads expensive next to a Meta ad and cheap next to local experiential, so quoting it first invites the wrong comparison — bring it out when they ask, because it proves the arithmetic was done. The real pitch is twelve trusted local businesses putting a sponsor's name in front of their own customers, plus first refusal on every year after this one.

**Confirm before quoting:** the shops' actual average follower counts and DripMap's own reach. Both are estimates in the model today.
